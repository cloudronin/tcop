\section{Problem, Goals, and Threat Model}

\subsection{System model and design goal}

We consider independently governed authorization boundaries that run agents or automation with local observers and enforceable local choke points. Domain A observes an event involving its own agent, workload, or telemetry. Domain B owns a downstream tool, resource, or gateway for a pending agent action. Domains may authenticate one another, but they do not share a policy engine or an enforcement API. Identity federation establishes who signed evidence; it does not confer authority to act in another domain.

TCOP permits runtime evidence observed in Domain A to influence a timely, scoped, and auditable Domain-B authorization decision for that pending action. The receiver can reject evidence, record a monitor-only disposition, restrict a narrow capability, or apply its own failure policy. The boundary follows the familiar policy-decision and policy-enforcement separation in zero-trust architectures while adding cross-domain runtime evidence \cite{nistZta,nistAbac}.

\subsection{Adversary and trust assumptions}

Agents, observers, and peer domains can be honest, compromised, or faulty. An adversary can relay, replay, delay, suppress, or reorder messages. An authorized peer can emit a syntactically valid but incorrect observation. A compromised client can present a valid receipt outside its intended session, and a stale local decision can survive a gateway restart unless the receiver checks current state. Domain B's enforcement boundary is trusted only to the extent that Domain B controls its policy and authorization adapter.

TCOP assumes a participating domain detects a useful fact, the receiver has an enforceable local agent-action choke point, and peer authorization plus public-key identity exist. The receiver binds accepted evidence to local state through a receipt or another locally governed mechanism. A signature authenticates a sender and protects the exchanged object; it does not establish observation truth or determine a response. This separation parallels remote-attestation architectures, which distinguish evidence from appraisal \cite{rats}.

\subsection{Evaluated properties and scope}

The protocol goal is not to make a remote observation self-executing. TCOP evaluates five receiver-side protocol properties: authenticated provenance; freshness and replay resistance; correlation and scope integrity; receiver sovereignty; and failure ownership. It evaluates these together with the non-retroactivity accounting rule.
